# Story and shot rules

## Short pilots

For three 8-second shots, give the viewer a readable attempt, a change of approach and a visual payoff. For a silent or effects-only brief, plan the movement and its ending without adding spoken words. Reserve the narration lengths below for spoken films.

## Narrated films
- Estimate calm narration at **1.8–2.2 words per second**, allowing about half a second for the ending to settle. Use word count divided by the estimated speaking pace, plus that margin, then round up to an available model duration. Treat this as a planning estimate and verify the actual audio.
- For an 8–10-word line, consider five or six seconds. Longer lines or deliberate pauses can require seven seconds or more. For a fixed four-second shot, aim for roughly 6–7 words with room for pauses. Show proposed wording changes before replacing approved narration.
- 2 minutes = 14 to 16 shots. Average shot 8 seconds. Prefer 6 to 10 seconds for regular story shots, using durations offered by the selected model.
- Fit one or two short sentences to the selected duration. If narration needs more time, lengthen the affected shot within the creator's authorization, or propose a sentence split. Preserve the exact approved words when extending. Update the shot timings and credit quote before submitting.
- If the creator approves five- or six-second shots after an initial four-second plan, apply that change where needed. Preserve successful takes that already fit unless uniform duration is explicitly requested. Report the resulting total runtime.
- Audio that cannot be heard remains unverified. Avoid cutting a clip's ending solely because a visual beat finishes early. A trim must preserve the spoken ending and its pause, or carry an explicit warning that audio completeness still needs checking.

## Story shape (2 minutes)
| Part | Shots | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | 1 | The strangest or highest-stakes moment, stated as fact. No setup. |
| Setting | 2 | Who, where, what they want. One concrete visual detail. |
| Escalation | 3 to 9 | 3 to 4 beats, each bigger: a try, a fail, a bigger try, a bigger fail. Others react. |
| Pivot | 10 to 12 | The real cause or a clever idea. Show it physically. |
| Payoff | 13 to 15 | The twist, landed on a visual. Last line is short and ironic. |
| Button | 16 (optional) | A silent 5 to 6 s reaction shot so the ending can breathe. |

## What works in Flow for these looks
- **One action per shot.** "He trips over the bucket and the paint splashes the wall" is one action. Three actions in one shot produce mush.
- **Exact counts.** "Two stick figures" not "some people". "One red bucket".
- **Prop connections.** State the attachment points and the actor. For a bird ringing a bell, use one continuous cord from the bell clapper to the bird's beak, with the bird perched below the bell. Check that the rendered bird pulls the attached cord and the bell moves.
- **Hands and hidden causes.** Give the observing character an unambiguous hand position away from the moving object. Keep the reveal outside the earlier frames. Describe the mechanism and direction of movement plainly.
- **Big readable props.** Ladders, buckets, doors, balloons, rockets, boxes, cakes, ropes. Avoid tiny objects and on-screen text.
- **Camera words Flow follows:** wide shot, medium shot, close-up, slow push-in, side tracking shot, low angle, over-the-shoulder, static locked camera. One camera move per shot.
- **Physical comedy reads; dialogue jokes don't.** Show the joke with a body reaction and a hold.
- **Silence:** for a shot with no narration write "No narration, no dialogue." and name the sound effects you do want.

## Narration style

When a narrator is requested, use the following approach.

- Third person, warm narrator. Present tense works best ("Ravi grabs the rope...").
- Short sentences, 6 to 14 words.
- Never describe what the viewer can already see in detail; add the meaning or the joke.
- End on the twist line. No moral, no "and that's the story".

## Check the reveal before approval
List, for each shot, which story objects are visible. If the payoff depends on a hidden cause (who rings the bell, what is behind the door), the cause and its object must stay out of frame until the reveal shot, and the prompt must say so ("the bell is out of frame"). In "The Last Bell" the bell was visible from shot 1, which weakened the crow reveal. Point this out in the plan review and ask the creator to read the story before approving it.
